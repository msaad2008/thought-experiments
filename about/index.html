What this is

Thought Experiments is five short games. Each one is built around a single idea from modern physics, the kind of idea that surprised scientists because it broke the rules they thought were solid. You don't read about the idea first. You play, and the game makes you run into it until it makes sense.

The five ideas

Antidote Run: special relativity

A clock moving relative to you runs slow, as measured by you. In the game, the ship's clock ticks slower than Earth's clocks during the trip, and when the ship comes home the crew really has aged less. The size of the effect is the factor γ = 1/√(1−v²/c²), where v is your speed and c is the speed of light. At everyday speeds γ is almost exactly 1, so you never notice it. Close to the speed of light it gets large.

What the game simplifies: the speed of light is shrunk in the game world, so trips take minutes instead of years.

Slingshot: gravity

Every mass pulls on every other mass. The pull gives an acceleration a = GM/r², where M is the mass of the thing doing the pulling and r is the distance to its centre. At a given distance from its centre, an object's pull depends only on its mass, not on its density. A denser object is just smaller, so you can fly closer to it, where the pull is stronger. The last levels add black holes, using a real approximation from general relativity, Einstein's theory of gravity.

What the game simplifies: gravity is the Newtonian kind everywhere except the black-hole levels, and everything happens in 2D.

Entangled Maze: entanglement

Two entangled particles share one quantum state. Each particle's own result is completely random, a 50/50 coin flip. Only when you compare both results does a pattern show up: for the pair in the game, the chance of getting opposite results is P(opposite) = cos²((a−b)/2), where a and b are the angles you measure at. Nothing travels between the particles. No signal and no motion goes from one to the other, and because each result alone looks random, you can't use entanglement to send a message.

What the game simplifies: the game uses an ideal pair of spin-½ particles (the half-angle in the formula comes from spin) and perfect detectors.

Uncertain Fire: the uncertainty principle

You can't know both where a particle is and how fast it is moving as exactly as you like. The limit is Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2, where Δx is the uncertainty in position, Δp is the uncertainty in momentum (mass times velocity), and ħ is a tiny constant of nature. Squeeze one down and the other has to grow.

What the game simplifies: ħ is made enormous so that the effect matters for something as big as a ship.

Collider: energy becomes matter

Energy and mass are two sides of the same thing: E = γmc². When particles collide at high speed, some of their energy can turn into brand-new particles. Not every outcome is allowed, though. Conservation laws, such as keeping the total electric charge the same, decide what can come out. Total energy, momentum and charge are always conserved.

What the game simplifies: there is only a small set of particles, and most collisions have two particles coming out.

For teachers

The games are free and need no sign-up. No accounts, no cookies, nothing that identifies anyone; we only count anonymous visits and finished levels. They work on phones and computers, and each one takes about 15 to 20 minutes.

Add ?classroom to any link, for example thoughtexperiments.app/?classroom, to unlock every level and save nothing. That suits shared school computers, where you don't want one student's progress showing up for the next.
